A passport is a small book with one job: it asks a foreign state to let in a person whom another state vouches for. Almost everything in it serves that request, which is why passports from opposite ends of the world look so alike.

## How a passport is laid out

**The cover.** Most covers carry three things, usually from top to bottom: the name of the issuing state, its emblem, and the word for the document, in the state's own language and often in a second one beneath. Lettering and emblem are pressed in gold or silver foil. Many modern covers add a small symbol near the bottom edge: a rectangle crossed by a bar, with a circle at its centre. ICAO, the United Nations agency for civil aviation, calls it the Chip Inside symbol, and its Doc 9303 allows it only on documents that carry an electronic chip built to its specification. It tells a border officer that the booklet can be read electronically.

**The data page.** This is the page with the holder's details. It names the kind of document and the state that issued it, gives the booklet its own number, and describes the holder: surname and given names, nationality, date of birth, sex and place of birth. Then come the dates of issue and expiry and the issuing authority. Each field answers a question an officer might ask. Who is this? Who vouches for them? Is this booklet still valid? The portrait is the largest thing on the page because a face is what the officer actually compares. The signature is the holder's own contribution to a page the state fills in. Doc 9303 allows a "usual mark" in its place, for holders who cannot write.

**The machine-readable zone.** At the foot of a real data page sit lines of plain, evenly spaced capitals. They repeat the essentials from the page above in a form a scanner can read at a glance. ICAO began work on the idea in 1968 with a practical aim: getting passengers through passport control faster. A machine also makes fewer copying mistakes than a tired officer at four in the morning. Passport does not draw a machine-readable zone. A decorative strip stands in its place, and the last section of this guide explains why.

**The chip.** An electronic passport has a small contactless chip sealed inside the cover or the data page. It stores a copy of the printed details and the portrait, signed digitally by the issuing state. The point is comparison: the officer's reader checks that the printed page, the stored data and the person at the desk all agree.

**The visa pages.** The rest of the booklet is numbered pages, left blank for other states to fill with visas and stamps. One page is usually set aside for official observations, where the issuing authority can add a note: a restriction, a correction, a change of name. Together the pages make the booklet a record as well as a request: where the holder has been, and who agreed to it.

## Security features and what they are for

A real identity document is built in layers, so that anyone trying to copy or alter it has to beat every layer at once. ICAO's Doc 9303 and the Council of the European Union's PRADO glossary describe them openly, because officers and the public need to recognise them. Here is what the common ones are meant to make hard.

- **Guilloche.** Fine, interlaced curving lines that form bands and rosettes, as on banknotes. They are meant to be too fine and too regular to redraw or copy cleanly. Because the pattern runs under the text and into the portrait, a change to either disturbs it.
- **Microprint.** Text so small that it looks like a thin line until you use a magnifier. On a casual copy the letters blur into each other.
- **Watermark.** An image formed in the paper itself while the paper is made, seen when the page is held to the light. It makes ordinary paper a poor substitute. Giving the data page a different watermark from the visa pages makes a swapped page stand out.
- **Colour-shifting ink.** Also called optically variable ink. It shows one colour head-on and another at a tilt. A copier or a camera sees a single angle, so a copy stays one colour.
- **Laminates and holographic overlays.** A thin clear film sealed over the data page, often carrying an image that shifts and flashes as it moves. It is meant to make it hard to alter the portrait or the details without leaving visible damage.
- **Laser-perforated numbers.** The document number burned through the pages as rows of tiny holes. It ties every page to its booklet, so a page taken out or put in is noticed.
- **Ghost image.** A second, smaller, fainter copy of the portrait elsewhere on the page. It makes swapping the photograph hard, since both likenesses would have to change and still match.
- **UV-reactive ink.** Printing that stays invisible, or changes colour, until it is put under an ultraviolet lamp. Security paper also stays dull under that lamp where office paper glows, so one look checks both ink and paper.
- **Intaglio.** Printing from an engraved plate under heavy pressure, which leaves the ink standing slightly proud of the paper. You can feel it with a fingertip, and flat printing has no relief to feel.

Passport borrows the look of the printed ones: guilloche rosettes, wave lines, lattice, rings and microtext. On screen they are decoration. They protect nothing, and are not meant to.

## Identity cards

Card sizes come from an international standard, ISO/IEC 7810. Its ID-1 size, 85.60 by 53.98 mm, is the size of a bank card, and most national identity cards use it. Its ID-3 size, 125 by 88 mm, is the passport booklet.

A card has two faces and no pages, so everything has to be rationed. The front usually carries what a person checking it needs first: the state's name and emblem, the portrait, the holder's names and date of birth, the card's number and expiry date, and often the signature. The back takes the rest: an address or place of birth, the issuing authority, a personal number and, on cards that can be used for travel, the machine-readable lines. Passport draws both faces, with its decorative strip where those lines would be.

Cards differ from passports in purpose as much as in size. A passport is addressed to foreign officials. An identity card is mostly for use at home: proving your age, opening an account, voting, collecting a parcel. It has nowhere to put a visa or a stamp. ICAO's Doc 9303 says plainly that it is not meant as a standard for national identity cards, and applies only where a card is also accepted for travel.

## Stamps and visas

A visa is permission given in advance. The state you mean to visit examines your request before you travel and, if it agrees, adds a visa to your passport: once a stamp, later a printed sticker filling a page, now often an electronic record. It says what you may do, for how long, and how many times you may enter.

An entry stamp records the moment that permission is used: the border post, the date, and sometimes the conditions of the stay. An exit stamp records the departure. Read as a pair, they show how long the holder stayed.

Shapes follow local custom, not a world rule, though custom can be systematic. The stamps of Europe's Schengen area were a tidy example: square corners for entry, rounded corners for exit, an arrow pointing in or out, and a small pictogram (aeroplane, ship, car or train) for the way you arrived. Elsewhere you will find ovals, circles, triangles and hexagons. Inks are usually black, blue, red, green or violet, and a few states use colour to tell arrivals from departures. Real stamps are rarely crisp. The pad runs dry, the officer's hand turns, one stamp lands on top of another.

Stamps are also fading out, as border records move into databases. The European Commission's Entry/Exit System began operating in October 2025 and, from 10 April 2026, replaced manual stamping for non-EU visitors on short stays. That leaves fiction as one of the few places where a full page of stamps is still being issued.

## A short history of the passport

Medieval rulers issued safe conducts: a written promise that a named envoy, merchant or pilgrim could cross their lands unharmed. In England the Safe Conducts Act 1414, passed under Henry V, made breaking a truce or a safe conduct high treason. It is often cited as the first mention of such papers in English law, and HM Passport Office's own history begins there. The Act protected safe conducts; it did not invent them.

The word came later. English took it from the French passeport, from passer, to pass, and port, and it was in official use by the sixteenth century. Whether the port was a harbour or a city gate (porte in French) is still argued over.

For much of the nineteenth century the trend ran the other way. John Torpey, in The Invention of the Passport, describes a broad move towards freedom of movement, with the North German Confederation dropping its passport requirements in 1867. Railways usually get the credit: trains carried more people across more borders than officials could inspect. Passports were not generally required for international travel until the First World War.

The war brought them back. In 1914 France, Germany and Italy made passports compulsory, and others soon followed. Britain's first modern passport, issued under the British Nationality and Status of Aliens Act 1914, was a single sheet folded into eight, with a photograph, a signature and a written description of the holder's face. Travellers disliked having their noses officially assessed. The controls were supposed to be temporary.

They were not. In October 1920 the League of Nations held the Paris Conference on Passports, Customs Formalities and Through Tickets. The 22 governments represented agreed on a uniform passport of what they called the international type, in booklet form, and Britain's dark blue booklet of 1920 followed it. The delegates hoped passports could be abolished once conditions allowed, and a further conference in 1926 was still debating it. The booklet outlasted the hope.

In 1922 the League approved a document for people who had no state to issue them one. The Nansen passport, named after Fridtjof Nansen, the League's High Commissioner for Refugees, went first to Russians left stateless after the revolution and then to other refugees. UNHCR records that more than 50 countries came to recognise it and that about 450,000 people received one.

Later the work of standardising passed to ICAO. In 1980 it published the first edition of Doc 9303, titled A Passport with Machine Readable Capability, which Australia, Canada and the United States used for the first machine-readable passports.

The chip came next. Malaysia is usually named as the first state to issue a passport with a biometric chip, in 1998. ICAO's own specifications for chips entered Doc 9303 in 2006, the year the United Kingdom issued its first electronic passport, and many other states followed within a few years.

## Designing papers for a fictional state

Start with what the state wants to say about itself. A passport is the one piece of official design that a state sends abroad in its citizens' pockets, and everything on it is a claim: how old the state is, what it holds sacred, which language comes first. An old empire might want its full ceremonial title, every word of it, in foil.

**Languages and their order.** Real passports usually print each label in the national language first and then in a second language for readers abroad. In your world, which language sits on top is a political fact. A conquered province's tongue may come second, or small, or not at all. Passport sets the document's title in up to two languages. The order is yours to argue about.

**Cover colour.** Real covers cluster in four loose families: reds and burgundies, blues, greens and blacks. Members of the European Union mostly chose burgundy, green is common among Muslim-majority states, and black turns up where it is a national colour. None of this is a rule. The trade publication Keesing Platform notes that nothing official governs cover colour, and that dark shades are popular partly because they look official and hide dirt. So choose a colour for a reason inside your world: the dynasty's livery, the flag, a famous dye.

**The emblem.** Use the nation's coat of arms if it has one, or one of Passport's simple emblems if it does not yet. On a cover the emblem is pressed in a single foil colour, so bold shapes with clear outlines work best.

**The calendar and dates.** If your world counts years its own way, its papers should too. Decide how a clerk would write a date of birth: day first or year first, the month as a number or a name, the era spelled out or abbreviated. Then be consistent, because bureaucracies are. A document valid for exactly ten years says something different from one that expires at the next coronation.

**Who gets a passport at all.** For most of history, most people never held one. Your state might issue internal passports that tie people to a district, or demand an exit visa before a citizen may leave. Papers might belong only to nobles, to the members of a guild, or to the citizens of one free city. Who is refused papers tells as much as who holds them.

**Stamps as storytelling.** A page of stamps is a journey. Entry at a mountain pass in spring, exit from a southern port in autumn: the reader fills in the story between them. Vary the shapes and the inks, tilt them a little, let one overlap another. Border posts are not tidy places.

**Small details.** Give the document a number in a pattern you could explain. Name the issuing authority, down to the office. Invent a holder with a plausible name and an unremarkable birthday. The most convincing fictional papers are slightly dull, as real ones are.

## Why Passport is for fiction only

Real identity documents protect real people. They are how someone proves who they are at a border, a bank or a hospital, and they only work while they are hard to fake. A convincing imitation of a real document can be used to harm the person it imitates, and to deceive the people who rely on it.

So Passport keeps a clear line, with four rules that cannot be changed:

1. Every data page and card carries a SPECIMEN overprint, and it cannot be switched off.
2. No picture is kept. The portraits are the tool's own drawings. You may add a photograph or an emblem of your own for one visit: it stays in your browser, it is never in the link or the profile file, and a photograph always has SPECIMEN printed across it.
3. There are no real countries in the templates, and the tool declines to print a real country's name as the issuing state, as a document's title or on a stamp.
4. There is no machine-readable zone. Where a real passport has one, Passport draws a decorative strip that reads "FICTIONAL · NOT A TRAVEL DOCUMENT", with the caption "Decorative strip. Not machine readable."

Passport also offers no print-ready, actual-size output.

None of this gets in the way of worldbuilding. An invented state needs papers that feel right in a story, on a wiki page or at a gaming table. A character's face or your nation's own emblem helps with that, which is why you can add them; the overprint, the invented state and the strip no machine can read are what keep the result plainly fiction. We would sooner Passport be plainly a drawing tool for fiction than something that could be mistaken for more.
